CODEBOOK 2 — PROPOSED REVISION
Arash Tavangar

I kept the five topics from Codebook 2. My main suggestion is to keep scope, request type, and topic as three seperate decisions. I also clarified some examples and overlapping rules. These changes are for the group to review before using this version.

HOW TO USE THIS CODEBOOK

For each original 311 category name:
1. Decide whether it is IN or OUT of scope.
2. If IN, decide whether it is a CONDITION or a TRANSACTION.
3. If IN, give it one topic, even if it is a transaction.

For OUT labels, leave type and topic as N/A.

Keep the orginal label. If its meaning is unclear, flag it and explain what needs clarification. Do not automatically put it in OUT or Other.

1. IN OR OUT OF SCOPE

IN: The label concerns a neighbourhood issue, local infrastructure, a facility, or a resident-facing service related to these things.

OUT: The label concerns tax accounts, supplier administration, internal office work, intergovernmental correspondence, or system errors rather than a neighbourhood issue.

Examples:
- Garbage not collected: IN, because it concerns a local service problem.
- Lawn repair: IN, because it concerns the physical environment.
- Water tax: OUT, because it concerns tax administration.
- Receiving a postdated cheque: OUT, because it concerns payment handling.

Important points:
- Private property is not automatically OUT. An issue on private property can still affect the neighbourhood.
- A department name or prefix is not enough to mark something OUT. A municipal team may be responding to a neighbourhood issue.
- For a vague label such as “Transport and delivery,” check its meaning before deciding. Do not assume it is internal work.

2. CONDITION OR TRANSACTION

CONDITION: The label describes an existing problem or state of a place.

TRANSACTION: The label describes obtaining a service, permit, delivery, or routine work without stating an existing problem.

Examples:
- Fallen tree: CONDITION.
- Garbage not collected: CONDITION.
- Damaged bin: CONDITION.
- Bin delivery: TRANSACTION.
- Building permit application: TRANSACTION.

If a label describes both a problem and a request to fix it, choose CONDITION.

For routine maintenance, I suggest choosing TRANSACTION unless the label states a problem. For example, “Pruning work” alone does not tell us that a tree is damaged or dangerous. This changes the rule in Codebook 2 and needs group agreement.

A label like “Park furniture” tells us the subject, but not whether something is broken or newly requested. Flag unclear cases instead of guessing.

Transactions still receive topics. They are excluded only from the final condition-based Pareto comparison, not from all annotation and analysis.

3. TOPICS

Choose one topic for each IN label. Classify the subject of the label, not an imagined cause.

A. MAINTENANCE / CITY WORKS

Meaning: Construction, repair, or upkeep of buildings, facilities, trees, greenery, and physical structures.

Include: Building maintenance, lawn repair, tree work, park furniture, and structural road repairs.
Exclude: Work specifically about traffic controls, parking, waste, water systems, or noise. Use those topics instead.

Examples:
- Gazon-Réparation: lawn repair.
- Entretien d'édifices municipaux: municipal building maintenance.

Boundary example:
- Asphalte travaux: Maintenance, because the subject is work on the road surface. Possible traffic disruption does not automatically make it a Traffic label.

B. TRAFFIC ENGINEERING / PARKING

Meaning: Traffic control, movement, access, and parking for vehicles, cyclists, or pedestrians.

Include: Traffic lights, signs, street direction, speed bumps, parking, bicycle parking, and slippery roads or sidewalks.
Exclude: General structural repairs such as resurfacing, unless the stated subject is traffic control or access.

Examples:
- Sens de rue: street direction.
- Stationnement ou débarcadère pour personne à mobilité réduite: accessible parking or drop-off.

Boundary example:
- Trottoir glissant: Traffic, under the proposed rule that slippery travel surfaces belong here. The decision should not depend on guessing how much work is needed to fix it.

C. SANITATION / CLEANLINESS

Meaning: Waste, cleaning, pest problems, and water systems.

Include: Waste collection, bins, cleaning, infestations, sewers, drains, and water supply problems or services.
Exclude: Water taxes, which are OUT of scope; ordinary tree work without a pest issue; general repairs unrelated to sanitation or water.

Examples:
- Déchets-Non ramassés: garbage not collected.
- Collecte de résidus alimentaires: food-waste collection.

Boundary example:
- Puisard: this belongs to the water/drainage subject. The name alone does not tell us whether it describes a condition or transaction.

I suggest explicitly including water supply as well as drainage here. This needs group agreement because the current definition is less clear about water supply.

D. QUIETNESS / LIVING COMFORT

Meaning: Noise and requests directly related to noise. “Living comfort” does not mean every kind of neighbourhood problem.

Include: Noise complaints and in-scope noise-related permits.
Exclude: Issues that do not explicitly concern noise.

Examples:
- Bruit.
- 800-Bruits.

Boundary example:
- Permis-Bruit-Résidence: if IN, its topic is Quietness / Living Comfort, but its type is TRANSACTION because it concerns a permit.

E. OTHER

Meaning: An understood, relevant subject that does not fit any of the four topics above.

Include: IN labels whose subjects fall outside the four defined topics.
Exclude: OUT labels, labels that fit another topic, and labels whose meaning is still unclear.

Illustrative examples, not examples confirmed in our dataset:
- A neighbourhood social-support programme unrelated to the other topics.
- A local heritage-recognition programme that does not involve physical restoration.

Boundary example:
- A community programme specifically about recycling belongs to Sanitation / Cleanliness, not Other.

Codebook 2 reports no Other examples in its classified open-coding set. These illustrative examples should be checked against the agreed scope and replaced with actual examples if available. If several real labels in Other share a theme, consider adding or adjusting a topic.

WHEN TWO TOPICS SEEM POSSIBLE

Use the explicit subject before a general word like “repair.”
- Traffic light repair: Traffic.
- Sewer repair: Sanitation.
- Building repair: Maintenance.
- Cleaning a traffic sign: Sanitation, because the stated issue is cleaning.

Use the specific boundaries above first. If two topics still fit equally, use the original priority order:
Noise, then Traffic, then Sanitation, then Maintenance, then Other.

Record a short note for dificult decisions. Do not infer crime, damage, or a need for new equipment unless the label or documented meaning supports it.

BEFORE FINALIZING

I think the group should agree on these points:
- Keeping transactions when assigning topics.
- Treating routine maintenance as a transaction unless a problem is stated.
- The boundary between road maintenance and traffic.
- Including water supply under Sanitation / Cleanliness.
- How to handle vague labels and abbreviations consistently.
